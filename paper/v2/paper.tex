\begin{abstract}
\noindent Streaming foreign accent conversion systems are designed around a
lookahead budget, and studies of the latency--quality trade-off report
accuracy as a function of that budget. Such results are valid only if the
model's output depends on no more future input than the budget states. We
show that two common constructions violate this. First, masking
self-attention does not make wav2vec~2.0 or WavLM base causal: a positional
convolution admits 1.28\,s of future input and a feature-encoder
normalisation admits the whole utterance. Second, a lookahead mask applied in
every layer compounds with depth: in a 13-layer model built on a frozen WavLM
encoder, a nominal 40\,ms budget yields at least 220\,ms of effective future
context, and a nominal 640\,ms at least 2.8\,s. We give a perturbation test
that measures effective future context and two masking schemes for which it
equals the nominal budget exactly. Re-measuring a phonetic-normalisation
probe for accent conversion under these schemes changes the conclusion
substantially. Under per-layer masking, phone error rate falls by 63\% from 0
to 640\,ms of nominal lookahead. With exact budgets the reduction is 12.5\%
when the lookahead enters the first encoder layer and 3.7\% when it enters
only after the encoder, where nearly all of the gain is obtained within
80\,ms. Two secondary findings of the per-layer analysis, a stronger
lookahead response for canonical than for realized phone targets and a large
difference between encoders, do not survive exact budgets. We conclude that
lookahead results should be reported with a measured effective context.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Streaming foreign accent conversion (FAC) has been demonstrated at low
latency. PHONOS~\cite{phonos2026} operates with at most 40\,ms of lookahead
and reports an 81\% reduction in non-native accent confidence, and related
systems for streaming conversion and anonymisation operate under similar
budgets~\cite{tvtsyn2026, darkstream2025}. The lookahead budget is therefore a
primary design parameter of such systems, and a natural question is how much
accuracy each additional millisecond of future context buys.

Answering that question requires that the budget a model is labelled with is
the future context it actually uses. Two properties of current practice make
this assumption fragile. Self-supervised speech encoders contain components
outside self-attention that read future input, so masking attention alone does
not make them causal. And a lookahead mask applied identically in every layer
of a deep network extends the receptive field by the budget once per layer.
The latter is known in streaming speech recognition, where the look-ahead
latency of per-layer right context is recognised to aggregate over the layers
of the stack~\cite{zhang2020transformertransducer}. It is easily overlooked,
however, when a frozen pretrained encoder is masked to emulate a streaming
condition, because the nominal budget then describes the mask rather than the
model.

This paper began as a characterisation of the lookahead--accuracy trade-off
for streaming FAC using a phone-translation probe, and an earlier version
reported a steep, log-linear benefit of lookahead under per-layer masking. A
measurement of the probe's effective future context showed that the masks
compounded and that the nominal budgets understated the context used by a
factor of three to five at short budgets and more at long ones. The present paper reports that finding, the tools
that detect and remove it, and the corrected measurement.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item We identify two components of wav2vec~2.0 and WavLM base that leak future
      input under attention masking, give patches for both, and verify them by
      a truncation test (\S\ref{sec:causal}).
\item We show that per-layer lookahead masks compound in a frozen-encoder
      probe and quantify the inflation, using a perturbation test that
      measures the effective future context of a complete model
      (\S\ref{sec:mask}, \S\ref{sec:reach}).
\item We define two masking schemes whose effective context equals the
      nominal budget exactly, one placing the lookahead after the encoder and
      one in the first encoder layer, and verify both with the same test.
\item We re-measure the lookahead response of the probe under all three
      schemes with matched training, and show that the per-layer result
      overstates the benefit of lookahead by a factor of five to seventeen
      and that two secondary findings derived from it do not hold under exact
      budgets (\S\ref{sec:schemes}--\S\ref{sec:encoder}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2025streaming} present a streaming
non-autoregressive accent conversion system built on an Emformer encoder, and
PHONOS~\cite{phonos2026} targets substantially lower latency with bounded
future context. PHONOS, TVTSyn~\cite{tvtsyn2026} and
DarkStream~\cite{darkstream2025} originate from one group and combine
conversion with speaker anonymisation. TVTSyn reports both CPU and GPU latency
at a 60\,ms chunk, and DarkStream ablates its look-ahead over
$\{0, 140, 280\}$\,ms, selecting 140\,ms because the further 140\,ms improves
anonymisation accuracy by less than one percentage point.
StreamVC~\cite{streamvc2024} reports mobile operation without device details.
A survey~\cite{acsurvey2026} frames the problem space; streaming
anonymisation~\cite{streamvoiceanon2026, streamvoiceanonplus2026, e2eanon2024}
shares the latency constraint, and offline FAC~\cite{zhao2021fac,
accentron2022} and low-latency voice conversion~\cite{llvc2023} bracket the
design space.

In streaming speech recognition, Zhang et
al.~\cite{zhang2020transformertransducer} note that applying the same
right-context mask in every Transformer layer makes the look-ahead latency
aggregate over the layers. Our contribution is not that observation but its
measurement and consequences in a setting where it is easy to miss: a frozen
self-supervised encoder masked to emulate streaming, used as a probe of how
much future context a conversion task requires. None of the FAC systems above
is evaluated here, and no claim is made about their effective context.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task and latency model}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let $\mathbf{x}$ be a 16\,kHz waveform from a non-native speaker, encoded into
frames $\mathbf{z}_{1:T}$ at hop $h=20$\,ms. L2-ARCTIC~\cite{l2arctic2018}
annotates each utterance with a canonical phone sequence
$\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{can}}$ derived from the lexicon and a realized sequence
$\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{real}}$ transcribed from what the speaker produced. We
operationalize the phonetic-normalisation component of accent conversion as
the prediction of $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{can}}$ from accented audio,
\begin{equation}
f_\theta:\ \mathbf{x} \longmapsto \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{can}}.
\label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
This is a controlled proxy for one stage of FAC; the future-context
requirements of acoustic generation and prosody may differ.

A streaming system consumes audio in chunks of $c=Ch$ and may delay its
output by a lookahead of $L$, realized as $N_L=\lceil L/h\rceil$ frames. Under
a block-emission convention its end-to-end delay is
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{e2e}} = \underbrace{c + L}_{\talg} + \tcmp + \tbuf ,
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where \tcmp{} is per-chunk computation and \tbuf{} is input/output and jitter
buffering. Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp} holds only if the output for a chunk depends on
no input beyond $L$ after the end of the chunk. Verifying that condition is
the subject of the following subsections.

\subsection{Lookahead masking schemes}
\label{sec:mask}

With zero-based frame indices, chunk length $C$, lookahead $N_L$ and lookback
$N_B$ frames, the chunked lookahead mask permits query frame $i$ to attend to
key frame $j$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
i - N_B \;\le\; j \;\le\; (\lfloor i/C \rfloor + 1)\,C - 1 + N_L .
\label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
The upper limit is the last frame of the chunk containing $i$, extended by
$N_L$ frames. The mask is added to the pre-softmax attention logits; in WavLM
it is added to the gated relative position bias, which leaves the model's own
padding mask in effect. All experiments use $C=2$ ($c=40$\,ms) and $N_B=100$
(2\,s). We compare three ways of applying it to a model consisting of a
frozen encoder tapped at layer $\ell$ followed by a four-block probe.

\textbf{Per-layer.} Eq.~\ref{eq:mask} with lookahead $N_L$ is applied in every
encoder layer and every probe block. The bounds compose: a frame at layer $k$
attends to frames of layer $k{-}1$ that have themselves attended $N_L$ frames
ahead, so after $K$ masked layers the output at frame $i$ can depend on input
as far as approximately $i + K(N_L + C - 1)$. For $N_L=0$ the bound does not
grow, since every attended frame lies in the same or an earlier chunk. For
$N_L>0$ it grows linearly with depth.

\textbf{End-to-end.} The encoder is masked frame-causally (Eq.~\ref{eq:mask}
with $C=1$ and $N_L=0$), the first probe block uses the full $N_L$, and the
remaining blocks use $N_L=0$. An encoder frame then depends on no input
beyond itself, the first block extends each frame's context to the end of its
chunk plus $N_L$ frames, and later blocks, which attend only within the
current and earlier chunks, cannot extend it further. The effective future
context is exactly $(C-1+N_L)h$.

\textbf{Encoder-first.} The first encoder layer uses the full $N_L$ and every
later encoder layer and probe block uses $N_L=0$. By the same argument the
effective future context is again exactly $(C-1+N_L)h$, but the pretrained
encoder now builds its representation with the future in view.

The two exact schemes differ in a second respect that matters at $L=0$: in
the end-to-end scheme encoder frames are frame-causal, whereas in the
encoder-first and per-layer schemes they may also attend to the remainder of
their own chunk.

\subsection{Measuring effective future context}
\label{sec:reachtest}

Let $F$ denote the complete model, encoder and probe. We replace every sample
after a time $\tau$ by independent noise and locate the earliest output frame
whose relative change, $\lVert F(\mathbf{x})_t - F(\mathbf{x}')_t\rVert /
\lVert F(\mathbf{x})_t\rVert$, exceeds $10^{-4}$; floating-point noise in this
computation is of order $10^{-5}$. The interval between that frame and the
first input frame affected by the perturbation is the effective future
context. Because a frame's context depends on its position within the chunk,
the test is repeated for each alignment of $\tau$ within a chunk and the
largest value is retained. The probe is randomly initialised, since the
property under test is structural. Values obtained this way are lower bounds,
because the influence of sufficiently distant frames can fall below the
threshold. The test is run for every budget before each training sweep, and
the sweep is aborted if an exact scheme fails it.

\subsection{Making the encoder causal}
\label{sec:causal}

Masking self-attention in wav2vec~2.0 or WavLM base~\cite{wavlm2022, wav2vec2}
does not produce a causal encoder, because two components outside the
attention layers also read future input.

\textbf{Positional convolution.} The convolutional positional embedding is a
grouped convolution with kernel size $K_p=128$ and symmetric padding, so the
embedding added to frame $t$ is computed from frames $t-64$ to $t+63$, 1.28\,s
of future context. We replace the symmetric padding with $K_p-1$ frames of
left padding,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K_p-1} \mathbf{W}_k\, \mathbf{z}_{t-(K_p-1)+k},
\label{eq:posconv}
\end{equation}
retaining the pretrained kernel $\mathbf{W}$.

\textbf{Feature-encoder normalisation.} In the base checkpoints the first
layer of the convolutional feature encoder is followed by a group
normalisation with one group per channel, whose statistics are computed over
the entire utterance. We replace it with a cumulative normalisation,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mu_t &= \frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t} x_s,\qquad
\sigma_t^2 = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t} x_s^2 - \mu_t^2,\\
y_t &= \gamma\,\frac{x_t-\mu_t}{\sqrt{\sigma_t^2+\epsilon}} + \beta,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cumnorm}
\end{equation}
applied per channel with the pretrained affine parameters $\gamma$ and
$\beta$. The large checkpoints use layer normalisation at this position and do
not require the second patch. Both patches alter the function computed by the
pretrained encoder, which is then used frozen; every condition uses the same
patched encoder, so comparisons across lookahead are unaffected.

\textbf{Verification by truncation.} A causal encoder must satisfy
$F(\mathbf{x})_t = F(\mathbf{x}_{1:\tau})_t$ for every frame $t$ ending before
sample $\tau$. With $N_L=0$, a 4\,s input truncated at 2\,s and the last
$m=8$ frames excluded to cover the front end's receptive field, we compute the
relative $L_2$ change $\delta$ in the remaining frames and require
$\delta<10^{-4}$. Table~\ref{tab:causal} reports the four combinations of the
two patches. Neither patch suffices alone, and applying the cumulative
normalisation without the causal positional convolution increases $\delta$.
The value for the fully patched encoder lies at single-precision accumulation
level and varies with the library release ($4.58\times10^{-6}$ here,
$6.14\times10^{-6}$ with an earlier release).

The truncation test establishes causality at $N_L=0$ only. It cannot detect
the compounding of \S\ref{sec:mask}, which appears only for $N_L>0$; that is
the role of the test in \S\ref{sec:reachtest}.

\subsection{Probe and matched control}
\label{sec:probe}

The probe operates on the hidden states of encoder layer $\ell$ ($\ell=9$ for
WavLM base+, $\ell=2$ for wav2vec~2.0 base; \S\ref{sec:encoder}). It consists
of four pre-norm blocks of width 768, each applying eight-head self-attention,
a depthwise convolution of kernel size 31 with left-only padding followed by a
pointwise convolution, and a feed-forward layer of width 2048 with GELU
activation, all with residual connections and dropout 0.1. A final layer
normalisation and a linear layer produce posteriors over 37 symbols (36 phones
after stress marks are removed, and the CTC blank). The probe has 24.6\,M
parameters, is trained with the connectionist temporal classification
objective, and is decoded greedily. Padded positions are zeroed after every
sub-layer.

Replacing $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{can}}$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:task} with
$\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{real}}$ yields a realized-phone transcription model that
shares architecture, capacity, vocabulary, data order, seed and step count
with the canonical-phone model and differs only in its target tensor.

\subsection{Reported quantities}
\label{sec:quantities}

PER is the Levenshtein distance between hypothesis and reference divided by
the reference length, averaged over test utterances. The \emph{exchange rate}
is the slope of the least-squares fit
$\mathrm{PER}(L)=\alpha+\beta\log_2 L$ over $L\ge20$\,ms, in PER per doubling.
The \emph{repeatability floor} $2\sigma$ is twice the standard deviation of
test PER over complete reruns of a condition at identical seed and
configuration; differences below it are treated as unresolved. The
\emph{preference margin} of a model is its PER against the other target minus
its PER against its own.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Data.} We use the \texttt{KoelLabs/L2Arctic} release of
L2-ARCTIC~\cite{l2arctic2018}, 3599 phoneme-annotated utterances from 24
speakers of six native languages, excluding utterances shorter than 0.8\,s or
longer than 10\,s. The data are divided into speaker-disjoint sets stratified
by native language: one speaker per language for validation, one for testing,
and the remaining speakers for training. Test PER is computed on a fixed
subset of 400 test utterances.

\textbf{Training.} The encoder is frozen. The probe is trained for 1200 steps
with batch size 8, AdamW (weight decay 0.01), a one-cycle schedule with peak
learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ and gradient clipping at 1.0. The order of
training utterances is identical across conditions. All training ran on an
NVIDIA T4.

\textbf{Sweeps.} Under the per-layer scheme: a dense sweep of 21 budgets from
0 to 640\,ms with two seeds on the canonical-phone arm, 16 of those budgets on
the realized-phone arm, and a seven-budget wav2vec~2.0 sweep. Under the
end-to-end scheme we repeat each of these and add a seven-budget,
two-target, three-seed sweep, a repeatability experiment and a convergence
check to 6000 steps. Under the encoder-first scheme we run seven budgets
$\{0,20,40,80,160,320,640\}$\,ms with two seeds for WavLM and one for
wav2vec~2.0. Every sweep verifies that no two conditions share the same
effective budget.

\textbf{Repeatability floor.} Repeating four budgets
($L\in\{40,160,240,320\}$\,ms) four times each at identical seed and
configuration gives $\sigma=0.00146$ ($2\sigma=0.0029$) under the per-layer
scheme and $\sigma=0.00251$ ($2\sigma=0.0050$) under the end-to-end scheme,
each with 12 degrees of freedom. We use the floor of the scheme being
analysed.

\textbf{Compute.} Per-chunk compute was measured on an Apple M4 (four
performance and six efficiency cores, 16\,GB) and an ARM64 Linux host (four
cores, 3.8\,GB), with 28 to 50 timed repetitions after three warm-up
iterations.

\subsection{Lookahead response under the three schemes}
\label{sec:schemes}

Fig.~\ref{fig:schemes} and Table~\ref{tab:schemes} show test PER of the
canonical-phone probe against nominal lookahead. Under the per-layer scheme
PER falls from $0.4256$ to $0.1561$, a relative reduction of 63.3\%, at an
exchange rate of $-0.0453$ PER per doubling ($R^2=0.985$). Under the
encoder-first scheme it falls from $0.4238$ to $0.3707$, a reduction of
12.5\% at $-0.0072$ per doubling. Under the end-to-end scheme it falls from
$0.4517$ to a minimum of $0.4273$ at 320\,ms (5.4\%) and ends at $0.4352$
(3.7\%), at $-0.0033$ per doubling ($R^2=0.60$). Relative to exact budgets,
the per-layer analysis therefore overstates the endpoint benefit of lookahead
by a factor of five against the encoder-first scheme and seventeen against
the end-to-end scheme.

\textbf{End-to-end scheme in detail.} The dense sweep samples every 20\,ms to
360\,ms. No individual 20\,ms step exceeds the floor of $0.0050$ (the largest,
40$\rightarrow$60\,ms, is $1.2\times$ the floor), but the cumulative reduction
from 0 to 80\,ms is $0.0182$ PER, $3.6\times$ the floor and 4.0\% relative.
Beyond 80\,ms the curve stays within about one floor of its 80\,ms value and
rises slightly between 360 and 640\,ms. A three-seed sweep on seven budgets
confirms the shape: the relative reduction from 0 to 640\,ms is 2.8\%, 4.4\%
and 4.8\% across seeds.

\textbf{Convergence.} All conditions are trained for 1200 steps. To test
whether the shallow response reflects under-training, five budgets were
trained to 6000 steps under the end-to-end scheme. Test PER was $0.4183$,
$0.4078$, $0.3941$, $0.3892$ and $0.4053$ at 0, 40, 160, 240 and 640\,ms.
Longer training increases the benefit to 7.0\% at 240\,ms, but the response
remains shallow and 640\,ms is again worse than 240\,ms.

\textbf{Encoder-first scheme in detail.} The two seeds give endpoint
reductions of 14.2\% and 10.8\% and agree in shape, but differ by up to
$0.024$ PER at individual budgets (160\,ms), about five times the end-to-end
floor. The monotone decrease and the endpoint are well supported;
differences between adjacent budgets above 80\,ms are not resolved.

\subsection{Where the context enters}
\label{sec:where}

Two comparisons indicate that the location of future context matters as well
as its amount. First, at $L=0$ the only difference between the encoder-first
and end-to-end schemes is that encoder frames may attend to the remainder of
their 40\,ms chunk, one additional 20\,ms frame for half of them. This alone
reduces PER from $0.4517$ to $0.4238$, a 6.2\% reduction, which exceeds the
entire benefit of 640\,ms of lookahead under the end-to-end scheme. Second,
the encoder-first scheme continues to improve at 640\,ms, whereas the
end-to-end scheme is flat beyond 80\,ms.

Amount nevertheless dominates the per-layer result. The per-layer model at a
nominal 20\,ms, with at most 520\,ms of effective context, reaches $0.3700$;
the encoder-first model with a full 660\,ms reaches $0.3707$. The steep
per-layer curve thus reflects effective contexts of several hundred
milliseconds to several seconds distributed through all thirteen layers,
which neither exact scheme provides at these budgets.

\subsection{Canonical against realized targets}
\label{sec:rq3}

Under the per-layer scheme, the canonical-phone arm gained $1.55\times$ as much
per doubling of lookahead as the realized-phone arm ($-0.0452$ against
$-0.0292$), the PER gap between the arms widened from $0.017$ at $L=0$ to
$0.094$ at 640\,ms, and the canonical arm's preference margin grew
monotonically in three of three seeds. The earlier version took this as
evidence that conversion requires more future context than accent-faithful
transcription.

Under the end-to-end scheme the effect is not resolved. On the dense sweep the
relative reduction from 0 to 640\,ms is 3.7\% for the canonical arm and 2.4\%
for the realized arm, with best reductions of 5.4\% and 3.9\%. On the
three-seed sweep the per-seed reductions are 2.8, 4.4 and 4.8\% for the
canonical arm against 1.9, 3.5 and 7.8\% for the realized arm, so the arms
overlap. The canonical arm's preference margin changes from $0.029$ at
$L=0$ to $0.033$ at 640\,ms and the realized arm's from $0.040$ to $0.036$;
both changes are within the floor. We therefore withdraw the claim. Whether
it holds under the encoder-first scheme, which was not run on the realized
arm, remains open.

\subsection{A second encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

The wav2vec~2.0 base layer is selected by phone decodability at $L=0$ under
the end-to-end scheme: PER is $0.438$, $0.465$, $0.564$, $0.699$ and $0.775$
at layers 2, 4, 6, 8 and 9, so layer 2 is used. Under the per-layer scheme
this encoder appeared to respond very differently from WavLM, with a 3.9\%
endpoint reduction against 63.3\%. Under that scheme, however, the two taps
sit behind nine and two masked encoder layers respectively, so at equal
nominal budgets they received very different effective context.

Under exact budgets the two encoders behave alike. In the end-to-end scheme,
wav2vec~2.0 reaches its best PER at 80\,ms, a 4.0\% reduction from $0.4357$ to
$0.4183$, and then degrades to $0.4544$ at 640\,ms; a second seed and a
6000-step run show the same rise beyond 160\,ms. In the encoder-first scheme
it is flat, with a best reduction of 1.6\% (single seed). The large
difference between encoders reported for the per-layer scheme was therefore
mostly a difference in effective context.

\subsection{Compute}
\label{sec:compute}

Across $L\in[0,640]$\,ms at a 40\,ms chunk, median per-chunk compute of the
WavLM model varied by $-0.3\%$ to $+1.9\%$ on the M4 and by 0 to $+7.6\%$ on
the ARM64 host, while the nominal algorithmic term varied seventeenfold. The
throughput condition $\mathrm{RTF}=\tcmp/c<1$ is not met at this chunk size
on the M4 ($\tcmp\approx43$--44\,ms), independently of $L$. These
measurements were taken under the per-layer scheme; the exact schemes apply a
nonzero lookahead in fewer layers and are not expected to cost more.

\section{Measurement audits}
\label{sec:lessons}

\textbf{Compounding lookahead.} The central error of the earlier analysis is
described above. It passed a truncation test, because that test ran at
$N_L=0$, where nothing compounds, and it produced a smooth and reproducible
curve ($R^2=0.985$, replicated across seeds and a denser grid).
Reproducibility of a curve says nothing about whether its abscissa is
correct. The guard we now apply is to measure effective context at every
budget before training.

\textbf{A padding error changed the shape of the curve.} An earlier version
of the probe passed a zero-padded tensor to a depthwise convolution and
feed-forward layer that ignore the padding mask. The resulting error grew
with lookahead, from $-0.014$ PER at $L=0$ to $-0.086$ at 320\,ms, so it was
not a constant offset. All results here postdate the correction.

\textbf{A forced breakpoint estimator returns a breakpoint.} A two-segment fit
selected by $\Delta$BIC~\cite{kassraftery1995} and a
maximum-distance-to-chord estimator both reported confident knees on the
per-layer curve, at 40 and 160\,ms, although the curve is log-linear. On
synthetic data that is log-linear for $L\ge20$\,ms with $L=0$ off the
extrapolation, including $L=0$ yields $\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=-39$ and a knee at
40\,ms, and excluding it yields $+5$. Identifiability must be tested by
perturbing the axis.

\textbf{Silent symbol mismatch.} A phoneme-class map keyed on ARPAbet, applied
to IPA output, sent 57\% of reference tokens to an ``other'' class and still
produced per-class results. A pre-specified manner-class analysis of the
per-layer data, conducted after this was corrected, did not support the
hypothesis that vowels and approximants benefit more from lookahead than
obstruents. We do not repeat it under exact budgets, where the total benefit
is only a few times the floor.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Phone-level probe.} All quality results are phone error rates of a
phone-translation probe over a frozen encoder, not perceived accentedness of
converted speech, and the requirements of synthesis and prosody may differ.

\textbf{Frozen encoder and scheme coverage.} The patched encoder is used
without adaptation. A fine-tuned encoder, or one trained for streaming, may
exploit future context differently. The two exact schemes are two of many
non-compounding designs; a budget distributed across layers, or a dedicated
right-context block, was not tested.

\textbf{Statistical resolution.} Most sweeps use two seeds and the
encoder-first wav2vec~2.0 sweep one. The repeatability floor is global, while
the per-condition spread varies, and the encoder-first seeds disagree by up to
five times the floor at individual budgets.

\textbf{Effective-context bounds.} Per-layer effective contexts are lower
bounds at a fixed threshold; \S\ref{sec:where} uses the theoretical upper
bound where the measured value is not sufficient.

\textbf{Second encoder.} The layer selected for wav2vec~2.0 is shallow,
leaving a single encoder layer above the encoder-first lookahead.

\section{Conclusion}

A lookahead budget describes a streaming model only if the model's output
depends on no more future input than the budget states. In a frozen-encoder
probe for streaming accent conversion, two conditions for this fail by
default: masking attention leaves the encoder non-causal, and a per-layer
lookahead mask compounds with depth, so that a nominal 40\,ms corresponded to
at least 220\,ms and a nominal 640\,ms to at least 2.8\,s. Both are detected by
simple input-perturbation tests and both have direct remedies. With exact
budgets, the probe's error rate falls by 12.5\% over 640\,ms when the
lookahead enters the first encoder layer and by 3.7\% when it enters after the
encoder, with most of the latter obtained within 80\,ms, against 63\% under
per-layer masking. Two secondary conclusions drawn from the per-layer
analysis, a target asymmetry and an encoder dependence, do not survive.
Studies of the latency--quality trade-off in streaming speech processing
should report a measured effective context alongside the nominal budget.
